Created by Bodhi Debnath for Terra Studio. Version 1 (November 2026).

The figures, places and names in the examples below are made up to show each pattern. Don't copy them into your own work as facts.

Demolish the AI slop in the text the user points to.

## What to target

- A **file** → edit it in place, then show the kill log.
- **Pasted text** → return the rewrite, then the kill log.
- **Nothing named** → work on the document currently in play, or the last substantial thing written in this conversation. If that is unclear, ask which.

This is a demolition pass. Cut hard. When a sentence and its slop depend on each other, delete both and rewrite the point in one plain line. Length should drop: aim for 15 to 30% fewer words unless the text is already lean.

## The one rule

Write like a smart human talking to another smart human: direct, specific, warm, opinionated. Concrete nouns and real verbs. One idea per sentence. Make the recommendation; skip the tour of every possible recommendation. If you wouldn't say it out loud to a sharp colleague, cut it.

## The kill list

Each pattern: why it's slop, then ❌ slop → ✅ human.

**1. Telling the reader how to feel about your own point.** The reader decides what's clever. State the thing and let it land.
❌ "This is the single most important chart in the report, and it took ten minutes to make." → ✅ "The chart took ten minutes to build from the city's open emissions data."
❌ "Here's the key insight:" / "The beauty of this is…" / "What's powerful here…" → ✅ (delete; just say the insight)
❌ "Most importantly," "crucially," "it's worth emphasizing that" → ✅ (delete. If it's the most important point, its placement shows it.)

**1b. The secret-knowledge flex.** A subspecies of #1 that also flatters the writer: framing an ordinary fact as hidden wisdom the smart reader is lucky to receive.
❌ "The unlock most people miss," "what nobody tells you," "the real reason," "here's the thing," "few people realize" → ✅ delete the flex and keep the fact: "Cement production accounts for about 8% of global CO2 emissions."

**1c. The dramatizing kicker.** A tacked-on absolute that inflates the point you just made and adds no information. It is usually the last sentence, and it dismisses everything around the claim to make the claim feel bigger. If the real point already landed, the kicker is theatre. Cut it and leave the gap empty.
❌ "…what's missing is a department that owns the heat plan and a first project. Everything else in the plan is theatre." → ✅ "…what's missing is a department that owns the heat plan and a first project."
❌ "This one number decides the whole strategy." / "Nothing else on this slide matters." / "This changes everything." / "If you take one thing away, it's this." → ✅ (delete. The emphasis belongs to the writer, and the reader didn't ask for it.)
This includes the rhythmic turn at the end of a paragraph: a final clause built for cadence with no content, usually "except," "and that's," "which is," or a repeated word swinging back on itself. If the paragraph already made its point, end there.
❌ "The city owes us the flood-map data, and we owe nothing except a follow-up with the planner who already turned us down." → ✅ "The city owes us the flood-map data. We owe the planning office a follow-up."
❌ "…the funder hadn't planned to mention the open call. That's the cost of not having a newsletter." → ✅ "…the funder hadn't planned to mention the open call."

**1d. Rating your own work or your own experience.** A superlative applied to the thing you just produced or the meeting you just had: "the most useful hour I've had on this," "the best session of the cohort," "one of the strongest submissions I've seen," "our sharpest brief yet." The reader wants the facts that would let them judge. The rating is the writer's opinion of themselves wearing an evidence costume. State what happened and let the reader rate it.
❌ "A peer review session, and the most useful hour I've spent on this policy brief so far." → ✅ "A peer review of the policy brief with two people from the city's housing team."
❌ "This was an unusually productive session." → ✅ "We closed three of the four open questions."
The tell: delete the superlative and no fact is lost.

**1e. The persuasion kicker, and the unsourced superlative.** A paragraph's final sentence that ranks, weights or dramatizes what the paragraph already said, written while arguing for a recommendation. It survives every keyword scan because it contains no banned word, so it needs a positional test.

**The last-sentence test.** For every paragraph, ask whether the final sentence carries a fact that appears nowhere else in that paragraph. If it only re-weights what came before, delete it.

❌ "…until we know whether the utility will share its smart-meter data. You told me you expect them to say no. Nobody has asked them yet, and that one answer changes the budget more than anything else." → ✅ "…You told me you expect them to say no. Asking them is the first thing we'd do in month one." (The replacement adds a fact: what happens in month one.)

Two tells mark it:

- **An unsourced superlative or comparison**: "more than anything else," "the biggest," "the single most," "the thing that decides this," "nothing matters more than." If you cannot point to the number or the quote the comparison came from, cut the comparison. Ranking three things you never compared is inventing evidence.
- **A gotcha about the reader's own omission**: "nobody has asked them yet," "which nobody owns today," "and that has never been tested." Pointing out a gap on their side to make your recommendation feel more necessary is persuasion wearing a fact's clothes. If the gap matters, state it once as a plain fact, and never as the closing beat.

**1f. Performed virtue: advertising your own character.** A clause that asserts something admirable about the writer's disposition: openness to criticism, candour, humility, willingness to be told no. The reader judges character from what you do. A claim about it buys trust cheaply and reads as a move.

❌ "Send me anything that looks wrong. I'd rather hear it now." → ✅ "Send me anything that looks wrong."
❌ "Four of the indicators you listed aren't in the dataset, and I'd rather say so now than after the report goes out." → ✅ "Four of the indicators you listed aren't in the dataset."
❌ "I'll be straight with you," "I'd rather lose the grant than overstate the impact," "I'm happy to be told I'm wrong," "no hard feelings if the answer is no," "I can't estimate the emissions honestly until…" → ✅ delete the clause and keep the fact.

Test: delete it. If no fact disappears and only the writer's self-portrait does, it was this.

**The distinction that matters.** Candour is good writing. "The survey sample is too small to say anything about rural households" and "we have never run this analysis at city scale" are candid sentences, and each one carries the uncomfortable fact. The banned version is the meta-statement about being willing to say or hear such things, with no fact attached. Say the uncomfortable thing. Never announce that you are the kind of person who says it.

**2. The salesy fragment triad.** Rule-of-three sentence fragments for cadence. Pure marketing throat.
❌ "Not a report. Not a campaign." / "Local. Data-driven. Urgent." → ✅ write one real sentence with a subject and a verb.

**2b. The count-announcement drumroll.** A bare number-noun fragment ("Three forces.", "Two things.", "Five reasons.") dropped in as a standalone sentence to tee up a list. It is a little drumroll that adds drama and no content. It has no verb; it just says "brace yourself, a list is coming." Either fold the count into a real sentence, or drop it and let the list speak.
❌ "Three forces. The heat wave created the urgency. The new federal funding…" → ✅ "Three things drove it. The heat wave created the urgency…" (a real sentence with a subject and verb), or delete the fragment and start at "The heat wave…".
❌ "Two problems here." / "The reasons are threefold." → ✅ say the first problem; the reader can count.

**3. "It's not just X — it's Y."** The fake-depth reframe. Almost always deletable.
❌ "This isn't just a dashboard — it's a movement." → ✅ say what it concretely does: "The dashboard shows each ward's tree cover next to its summer surface temperature."

**3b. The bare comma-contrast: "X, not Y."** The same tic as #3 with the "just" and the em dash stripped out, which is why it survives every keyword scan: the sentence contains no banned word. It appends a foil to a fact so the fact sounds sharper. It peaks in short formats (slide cards, social posts, headlines, course blurbs) where there's room for one sentence and the contrast is the shortest way to carry two ideas. **Delete the foil. Keep the fact.**
❌ "Communities make decisions, not suggestions." → ✅ "Residents vote on which projects get funded."
❌ "Funders want a number they can check, not a vision statement." → ✅ "Funders want a number they can check."
❌ "We hand over a working model, not a vendor contract." → ✅ "The city's own staff run the model after we leave."
Same for the variants: "rather than," "instead of," "and nobody," "never a." Also the two-fragment version: ❌ "No jargon and nobody left behind." → ✅ "Every technical term is defined the first time it appears."
Before you finish, search the draft for these strings and check each hit: `, not` · `rather than` · `instead of` · `n't just` · `not just`.

**4. Throat-clearing preambles.** "It's important to note that," "It's worth mentioning," "In today's fast-paced world," "In the realm of," "When it comes to," "At the end of the day," "Let's dive in / unpack / explore." → ✅ delete and start at the noun.

**5. Thesaurus hype.** leverage→use, utilize→use, delve→look at, harness/unlock/unleash→use/get, navigate→handle/work through, robust/seamless/holistic→(cut or be specific), empower/elevate/supercharge/turbocharge, game-changer, cutting-edge, best-in-class, world-class, tapestry, realm, testament to, stands as, at the forefront of. → ✅ plainer word, or none.

**5b. Abstract-jargon compression.** A plain idea packed into a clever-sounding abstract phrase, so it reads as insight when it is a simple thing. The tell: you can't picture it, and unpacking it into plain words makes it longer and clearer. Say the concrete thing, even if it takes more words.
❌ "a bounded first win" → ✅ "a first project small enough to finish"
❌ "reduce surface area," "a forcing function for X," "net-new capability," "step-change in velocity," "operationalize the learnings," "close the loop on ownership" → ✅ say what literally happens: "so there's less that can break," "a deadline that makes X happen," "something they couldn't do before," "shipping noticeably faster."
Test: if you wouldn't say the phrase out loud to a colleague without wincing, it's this.

**6. Vague intensifiers.** incredibly, truly, really, very, remarkably, significantly, deeply, fundamentally, transformative, revolutionary, powerful. → ✅ cut them. If the claim needs propping, the claim is weak: fix it with a number or an example.

**7. Hollow parallelism and aphorism.** Sentence-shaped nothing that sounds wise.
❌ "It's not about the data — it's about the people." / "Climate action isn't a destination, it's a journey." → ✅ delete, or replace with the specific mechanism.

**8. The restating conclusion.** "In conclusion," "To sum up," "In summary," and closing paragraphs that repeat the opening. → ✅ delete. End on the last real point.

**9. Both-sidesing.** "It depends," "there are pros and cons," listing options without picking. → ✅ make the call and give the reason. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real, and name what would resolve it.

**10. Hedge stacks and weasels.** "may potentially," "could possibly help to," "studies show," "experts agree," "it's widely known." → ✅ commit, or cite the source.

**11. Robotic signposting.** "Firstly… Secondly… Lastly," "Now, let's turn to," numbering things that aren't a sequence. → ✅ let paragraphs flow; number only true steps or rankings.

**12. Doubling.** Every noun gets a synonym twin: "clear and concise," "robust and scalable," "tools and capabilities." → ✅ pick the better one.

**13. Over-structuring.** Bullets where a sentence works, headers on two-line sections, bolding every other phrase, emoji bullets. → ✅ prose for connected ideas; bold only the rare term the reader must not miss.

**14. Filler verbs and phrases.** "in order to"→to, "due to the fact that"→because, "a number of"→some/many, "plays a key role in," "serves as," "is designed to." → ✅ shorten.
This includes verbs that make a deliverable sound accidental: "you end up with a climate action plan," "what comes out of it is," "there emerges." Name who produces it and what it is. ❌ "you end up with a draft climate action plan" → ✅ "you get a draft climate action plan" or "the workshop gives you a draft climate action plan."

**15. Gamified strategy-bro labels.** "The move," "the play," "first move," "the unlock," "the lever," "the secret weapon," slapped on as punchy nouns or headers to fake tactical crispness.
❌ "First move: call the city's sustainability officer." → ✅ "Do this first: call the city's sustainability officer." (or fold it into the sentence)

**15b. Framing instructions.** Telling the reader how to mentally hold the point in place of stating the point: "the frame to hold," "the lens here," "the mental model," "the way to think about this," "read this as." The fact carries its own weight. Delete the instruction and state the fact.
❌ "The frame to hold: the coal plant is scheduled to close in 2030." → ✅ "The coal plant is scheduled to close in 2030."
❌ "The way to think about this pilot is as a proof of concept." → ✅ "The pilot covers 200 homes and expands to the whole district if energy bills drop."

**16. Pet-word repetition and faux-precision tics.** A distinctive word repeated until it's wallpaper. The worst offender is "exact / exactly," then "really," "key," "huge," "clean," "strong." Usually hollow emphasis.
❌ "the exact approach… exactly what residents need… exactly as the model predicted." → ✅ vary or cut; "exactly" almost never adds information.
Rule: if any vivid word shows up four or more times in a document, it's a tic. Swap or delete most uses.

**16b. "is exactly when / where / what / how": the false-precision dramatizer.** A single-use cousin of the tic above: "exactly" glued to a relative clause to make an ordinary claim sound pinpointed and profound. It fakes precision the sentence doesn't have and adds a beat of drama. Almost always delete the word; the claim reads truer plain.
❌ "after a flood is exactly when people read the risk map" → ✅ "after a flood is when people read the risk map most."
❌ "the gap is local data, which is exactly what we collect" → ✅ "…which is what we collect." ❌ "that's exactly the argument for a heat-pump rebate" → ✅ "that's the argument for a heat-pump rebate."
Keep "exactly" only where a real quantity or match is being asserted ("emissions landed exactly on the 2025 target"), never as emphasis.

**17. "Where X lives / happens" evocative clichés.** "That's where the value lives," "where the magic happens," "where the real work gets done," "where X sits/breathes." Faux-evocative filler. State the fact.
❌ "The retrofit programme is where the emissions savings live." → ✅ "The retrofit programme delivers 60% of the plan's projected emissions cuts."

**18. Compressed shorthand that needs decoding.** A whole argument zipped into abstract noun phrases. Each phrase stands for a concrete thing the writer knows and never says. It reads smart and communicates nothing, because the reader has to know the idea already to unpack the sentence. Crisp, clear, simple writing beats anything fancy: name the actors, the amounts, the actions. If unpacking makes it longer, longer wins.
❌ "Funders back climate projects in single-approver steps. Pilot-sized asks that credit toward the scale-up close; big-bang proposals stall. Both paths are structured to start small and compound." → ✅ "A $20K pilot grant is something one programme officer can approve this quarter. A $2M programme needs a board vote, and board votes take months. So both proposals start with something small enough for one person to say yes to, and grow from what the pilot proves."
Test: read the sentence to someone who wasn't in the meeting. If they'd have to ask "meaning what?", decode it on the page with a number, a person, or a plain verb.

**19. Attribution theatre: "your words," "as you said," "your phrase."** Quoting things back at the reader to borrow their authority or flatter them. Two problems. It's a rapport move dressed as evidence. And when the source is a transcript or meeting notes, who-said-what is often wrong, and misattributing a quote to a real person in a shared document is worse than any slop. State the point without the attribution. Keep a quote only when you are certain of the speaker AND the quote does work the plain claim can't.
❌ "Emissions inventories are rarely complete — your words." → ✅ "Emissions inventories are rarely complete."
❌ "'Data before dashboards,' your phrase for the approach that works" → ✅ "'Data before dashboards' is the approach that works for small councils."
❌ "as you rightly said in our last call" → ✅ (delete; make the claim directly)

**19b. The callback flex: "the line that stuck with me."** The worst subspecies, and the one that reads most obviously as AI. A frame announcing that the writer was listening carefully and has selected a highlight, wrapped around a quote the recipient already knows they said. It flatters both parties and carries no information. Variants: "the thing that stuck," "what really landed," "the bit I keep coming back to," "one line from our call has been rattling around." **Delete the frame. Say the thing.** Attributing it in plain words is fine when you are writing to the person who said it.
❌ "Amira, the line that stuck with me was you saying residents stop reading after the first chart." → ✅ "Amira, you said residents stop reading after the first chart."
❌ "What really landed was how you described the gap between climate data and the people who need it." → ✅ "You described a gap between the city's climate data and the residents who need it. Our project is built around that gap."

**20. The cryptic headline: a title whose subject is an unnamed abstraction.** "The biggest number," "the real constraint," "where this breaks," "the piece nobody owns." The reader has to read the body to find out what the title is even about, so the title carries suspense in place of information. It is worse on a slide, a chart title or an email subject line, where the title travels alone: forwarded, skimmed, or read in a table of contents, it says nothing. Name the noun and the number, and put the claim in the title.
❌ "The biggest number sits with the 40% of households we cannot reach" → ✅ "Reach the 40% of households without home internet through libraries"
❌ "The thing that decides this policy" → ✅ "Council budget timing sets the rollout date"
❌ "Where the pilots died" → ✅ "Nine of twelve heat-pump pilots stopped at the permit stage"
Test: cover the body and read the title. If you cannot say what it claims, it is this.

**21. Theatrical verbs and borrowed metaphors.** A plain fact dressed in a vivid verb or an image imported from somewhere else: sport, war, sailing, poker, physics, construction. It reads as writing more than as information, and a senior reader hears the performance before they hear the point. Say what literally happens.
❌ "before the carbon tax bites" → ✅ "before the carbon tax takes effect" (or name the date)
❌ "data cleaning is the long pole" → ✅ "data cleaning takes the most time"
❌ "while the first cohort finds its feet" → ✅ "while the first cohort is still learning"
❌ "we do it on a clock that ends" → ✅ "the project has an end date"
❌ "the small utility will not win that auction" → ✅ "the small utility will not outbid them"
❌ "with real legacy weight underneath" → ✅ "with 30 years of paper permit records underneath"
❌ "with her own money on the line" → ✅ (cut; it adds nothing)
Also: "moving the needle," "the flywheel," "punch above," "double down," "the north star," "table stakes," "a step change," "lands," "bites," "gates," "unlocks."
Test: would you write this verb in a one-line chat message to a colleague? If it would look like you were trying, it is this.

## Built for the quick read (sentence architecture)

The kill list polices words. This section polices construction. The test for every sentence: a reader skimming at speed should never have to re-read it. Density is a tax the writer shifts onto every reader, and it doesn't make the writing smarter.

**Q1. One claim per sentence, links written out.** If the reader must hold two facts and infer why they're together, split them and write the connective ("so," "because," "which means"). A semicolon joining facts whose relationship is unstated is a claim smuggled past the reader.
❌ "The province and two utilities co-fund the heat-pump rebate; the city's constraint is installers, and its colleges run trade programmes." → ✅ "The city is short of heat-pump installers. Demand is covered, because the province and two utilities already co-fund a rebate."

**Q2. No mid-sentence asides.** An em dash or parenthetical insertion inside a sentence that already carries a decision forces a re-read. Promote the aside to its own sentence or cut it.
❌ "pilot the flood alerts in one neighbourhood — the riverside wards are the obvious pick — linked to the city's 311 system" → ✅ "Start in one neighbourhood. The riverside wards flood most often, so start there."

**Q3. Verbs over noun stacks.** Three or more nouns chained into a phrase read as a wall. Unpack them into what literally happens.
❌ "the full sensor-to-dashboard air-quality data pipeline across the school monitoring network" → ✅ "sensors at each school that send readings to a public dashboard every hour."

**Q4. No trailing modifier chains.** Decisions hung off a sentence's tail ("…, linked to X, with Y and Z as the baseline") each deserve their own sentence.

**Q5. Base fact before the conditional.** State the number as its own sentence, then the if-then.
❌ "If it cuts one week from the permit time per heat-pump install across a few thousand installs a year, the backlog clears within a year." → ✅ "The city approves a few thousand heat-pump installs a year. Cut one week from each permit and the backlog clears within a year."

**Q6. The reader's vocabulary.** Internal shorthand ("the MRV layer," "Scope 3 coverage," "the binding constraint," a term from your course or your team's meetings) assumes the reader was in the room with you. Use their words, or define the term at first use.

**Q7. Never stack a list inside a sentence.** Three or more items dropped after a colon or dash, each carrying its own modifier. The commas separate items and attach modifiers at the same time, so the reader cannot tell which job any comma is doing until they reach the end and work backwards. Break it into one sentence per item, or a real bulleted list.
❌ "you get a plan: the emissions sources ranked and checked against the 2023 inventory, which programmes replace the current rebates and what each department has to fund for them, the data gaps described well enough for the planning team to schedule, and the order the measures go in with a reduction target per year" → ✅ four bullets, or four sentences that each start with "It".

**Q7a. Give every item the same grammatical shape.** The example above runs noun plus participle, then a noun clause, then noun plus participle, then noun plus relative clause. Four shapes means the reader parses each item cold and can't pattern-match down the list. Pick one shape and hold it across the whole list.

**Q7b. Keep inputs and outputs in separate lists.** ❌ "Six weeks, about ten hours a week, and you end up with a published data story" reads as three of a kind. The first two are what it costs and the third is what you get, so they belong in different sentences.

This sits alongside the overcorrection guardrail below: short sentences for claims and decisions, longer sentences where the reasoning flows, and never three clipped fragments for cadence.

## Borrowed from Simplified Technical English

Three disciplines from ASD-STE100 that make prose more trustworthy. The rest of STE (20-word sentence caps, restricted vocabulary, no metaphor) stays out, and the guardrail below still governs.

**S1. Name the actor.** No actorless passive when a person did the thing. Passives hide who decided, who failed and who owes what, and hidden actors read as evasion.
❌ "the programme's budget was cut in half" → ✅ "the province cut the programme's budget in half"
❌ "the decision was made to delay the climate plan" → ✅ "the mayor delayed the climate plan"
Passive is fine when the actor is unknown or irrelevant ("the reading was flagged by the sensor's error check").

**S2. One name per thing.** Pick a term and repeat it. Rotating synonyms for elegance ("the programme… the fellowship… the initiative") makes the reader wonder whether these are three different things. This is the opposite of pattern 16: repeating a *technical term* is precision, and repeating a *vivid pet word* is a tic.
❌ "the pilot… the initiative… the effort… the programme" (all one thing) → ✅ call it "the pilot" every time.

**S3. Numbers over adjectives.** Where a quantity exists, use it. Size adjectives are opinions wearing a fact costume.
❌ "a small team" → ✅ "a 16-person team" · ❌ "a large grant" → ✅ "a $600K grant" · ❌ "soon" → ✅ "by mid-August"
If no number exists, say so plainly ("the agency hasn't published a figure") and skip "significant."

## Hard bans (specific words)

- **"actually"**: filler emphasis in nearly every use: "what decarbonization actually costs," "where pilots actually stalled," "to actually use it," "what the data can actually support." The sentence means the same without it; the word adds a smug beat of contrast with an imagined naive reader. Delete it. Keep it only when it marks a true reversal of a stated expectation ("we assumed X; actually, the logs show Y"), and even then "in fact" or a rewrite is usually cleaner.

- **"room / rooms"** as jargon: "get in the room," "warm rooms," "the room is the product," "fill the rooms." Use the word only when it means a physical room and no cleaner word exists. Say what you mean: "warm rooms" → "warm introductions" or "small peer groups"; "fill the rooms" → "fill the workshop."

- **"by Monday" / "by Friday" / "by end of week"**: the fake-urgency deadline, tacked on to make a question or task feel punchy. It's manufactured drama with no real date behind it. Cut it. ❌ "who owns it by Friday?" → ✅ "who owns it?" ❌ "My first step — by Monday" → ✅ "My first step". If a real deadline exists, name it. Otherwise drop it.

- **"load-bearing"**: an engineering metaphor dressed up as insight ("the load-bearing assumption," "that word is doing a lot of load-bearing work"). Say what depends on it. ❌ "verify the load-bearing assumptions" → ✅ "verify the assumptions the model depends on."

- **"arithmetic fiction" / "arithmetic friction"** (and cousins like "the math doesn't math"): a clever-sounding dismissal where a plain claim belongs. ❌ "at this install rate the 2030 target is arithmetic fiction" → ✅ "at this install rate the 2030 target doesn't add up," or state the gap in numbers.

- **Solicitous compliance**: advertising that you followed the brief: "everything you asked for," "just as you requested," "per your requirements," "as promised." Eager-to-please framing that puts the writer's obedience on display. Present the thing plainly and let it show that it answers the brief. ❌ "Everything you asked for, in five blocks" → ✅ "The 90-minute session, in five blocks."

## Soft bans (use only when no other word works)

These aren't hard-banned, but reach for them last; the plain alternative is almost always better. If one survives a pass, it should be because no other word does the job.

- **"honest"** as a qualifier ("honest debate," "honest conversation," "the honest part," "honestly"). Nobody advertises a dishonest debate, so the word adds nothing and quietly implies everything else was fake. ❌ "15 min · honest debate" → ✅ "15 min · debate". Acceptable only where it carries real contrast ("the honest answer is we don't know yet").

- **"shape" / "-shaped"** as vague abstraction: "the shape of the project," "the shape: we open with…," "validate the shape," "policy-shaped opportunity," "programme shape." The word gestures at a structure without naming it, so say the concrete thing. ❌ "The utility's pilot validates the shape" → ✅ "The utility already runs this kind of rebate." ❌ "The shape: we open with the survey" → ✅ "The flow: we open with the survey" (or start describing it). Acceptable in its literal sense (a physical shape, a curve on a chart) or as a plain verb with a concrete object ("zoning rules shape where solar farms go").

## Em dashes

Remove every em dash in the rewrite. Use a comma, a colon, parentheses or a full stop, or split the sentence. Paired em dashes used as brackets ("the report — the one we published — is live") are the strongest AI-cadence tell, and a single em dash before a punchline is the second strongest.

## The overcorrection guardrail

Killing slop is no licence for terse robot-speak. Three fragments in a row ("Quick. Structured. Clear.") is itself slop; see pattern 2. Keep warmth, voice, and a strong line the writer earned. Vary sentence length. Aim for a human who writes well, and stop short of a telegram.

## Output

1. The rewritten text (edit the file, or return the passage).
2. **Demolished:** a short log of the worst 5 to 10 offenders as `pattern → what you cut`, one line each. Don't pad this into its own slop.

No word counts. No commentary on how clean the text already was or how little needed cutting; that's grading your own homework. The measure of a pass is whether the result sounds like a person.
